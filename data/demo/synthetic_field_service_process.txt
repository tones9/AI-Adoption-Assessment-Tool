Synthetic Field Service Request Handling — SYNTHETIC DEMONSTRATION DATA

This synthetic demonstration process describes how a fictional equipment maintenance team handles an incoming service request from receipt through to scheduling. It was written to demonstrate the assessment engine on a process whose operational facts are documented. It is illustrative material only: it is not a real customer process, not research evidence, and not a record of any measured outcome.

1. A service coordinator sorts each incoming maintenance request by equipment type and urgency, then places it in the matching work queue in the Field Service System.

Operational facts recorded for sorting requests. The team sorts about four thousand requests every month and the task is carried out the same way each time. The sorting rules are stable and the correct queue can be determined from the request text in almost every case. Every request already carries a structured equipment code, a customer identifier and a free-text description, and three years of correctly sorted history is retained in the Field Service System. Assigning short free-text requests to a fixed set of queues is a well-established use of current classification technology. A coordinator applies no personal judgement when sorting a request. Faster sorting would save the team a moderate amount of handling time each month. A wrongly sorted request is delayed by a few hours and is corrected by the receiving queue. With a coordinator reviewing the queue each morning, the remaining risk of a request sitting in the wrong queue is negligible. Connecting a classifier to the existing queue interface is a contained piece of work for the platform team. A fixed keyword rule was tried in the past and failed on free-text descriptions, so a conventional rules-only approach is a poor fit for this activity. No individual is formally accountable for the sorting step itself.

2. A contracts administrator checks the request against the customer's service contract and records whether the requested work is covered, partly covered, or outside the agreement.

Operational facts recorded for the entitlement check. The team checks about one thousand five hundred requests every month against a stable set of contract templates. Entitlement wording changes rarely and the same contract clauses decide most cases. Reading a contract clause and assigning the request to a coverage category is a well-established use of current document and classification technology. An administrator applies limited personal judgement once the relevant clause has been located. Correct entitlement decisions protect a significant amount of recoverable revenue each month. A wrong coverage decision is corrected at invoicing and causes a short billing dispute. With an administrator confirming each coverage decision, the remaining risk of an incorrect invoice is small. Integrating a contract reader with the entitlement record is a moderate piece of work. Some straightforward cases could be handled by existing entitlement rules, so a conventional approach partly fits this activity. No individual is formally accountable for the entitlement check itself. The team has not recorded what condition the stored contract documents are in, whether historical coverage decisions are retained, or who controls access to them.

3. A scheduling planner drafts the scheduling note that is sent to the field engineer, describing the fault, the parts likely to be required, and the site access arrangements.

Operational facts recorded for drafting the scheduling note. The team drafts about two thousand scheduling notes every month. The content of a note varies with the fault and the site, so the wording is not predictable from the request alone. Past scheduling notes, parts lists and site access records are held in the Field Service System, and most fields are complete although free-text quality varies. Drafting a structured note from existing records and reference material is a well-established use of current text generation and retrieval technology. A planner applies substantial personal judgement about site access and likely parts. Better scheduling notes would materially reduce wasted engineer visits. An inaccurate note can send an engineer to a site without the right parts, which loses a working day. With a planner reviewing every draft before it is sent, some risk of a wasted visit remains. Connecting a drafting assistant to the parts and site records is a moderate piece of work. A template-only approach was rejected because notes vary too much between sites, so a conventional rules-only approach is a poor fit for this activity. A named planner remains formally accountable for every note sent to a field engineer.

4. A regional service manager approves or refuses a goodwill repair when the requested work falls outside the customer's contract.

Operational facts recorded for goodwill approval. The team receives about three hundred goodwill requests every month and each one is considered on its own merits. The outcome depends on the customer relationship and the circumstances of the failure, so it is not predictable from the request record alone. Past goodwill decisions, their stated reasons and the associated costs are retained in the Field Service System. Ranking a case against comparable past decisions is a plausible use of current decision-support technology. A manager applies substantial personal judgement weighing commercial and relationship factors. Consistent goodwill decisions would deliver a moderate saving on disputed repairs. A wrong goodwill decision commits unrecovered cost and can damage a long-standing customer relationship. Even with a manager reviewing every recommendation, the risk of an unjustified commitment remains material because the reasons behind past decisions are recorded inconsistently. Building a comparable-case tool over the goodwill record is a moderate piece of work. Existing discount rules cover only a minority of cases, so a conventional approach fits this activity only partly. A named regional service manager remains formally accountable for every goodwill decision.
